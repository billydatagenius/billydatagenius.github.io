From Classroom to Code: What 14 Years of Teaching Taught Me About Data

By Blessing "Billy" Bhobho — Founder, Billy Data Genius · Nelspruit, Mpumalanga

People imagine data science as a room of machines and equations. In practice, it's a room full of people who need a complicated thing explained before Friday. Which is to say: it's a classroom.

Seven schools, one syllabus: clarity

Between 2011 and today I taught in seven schools across Mpumalanga — Accounting, Business Studies, Computer Applications Technology, English, EMS, Mathematical Literacy. I carried marksheets in Naas, discipline files in Witbank, timetables in Hazyview, HOD folders in Dwasloorp. Different buildings, same lesson: the person who can explain complexity simply becomes the most useful person in the building.

That's the skill my clients actually hire. TensorFlow can find a pattern; only a human can make a principal, an owner or a manager believe it — and believe it in the two minutes they have.

"A model is only as persuasive
as the sentence that sells its insight."— fourteen classrooms of field-testing

What an HOD knows about governance

Being a Head of Department and chairing committees taught me the unglamorous half of data work: structure. Marks must be moderated. Records must be kept in one trusted place. Processes must survive the day you're sick. Strip the school vocabulary and you're describing data governance — reproducible pipelines, versioned numbers, one source of truth. I got twenty years of practice before my first SQL join.

Never actually left the classroom

While teaching full-time, I kept enrolling myself:

  • Jovian — Data Analysis with Python, Zero to Pandas
  • UniAthen — Executive Diploma in Data Analytics + Microsoft Power BI diploma
  • Cambridge PGDDSC — completed with Distinctions (2024)
  • WorldQuant University Applied Data Science Lab — Distinctions (2026); Computer Vision Lab in progress; Applied AI Fundamentals next
  • Stadio — Advanced Diploma in Technical and Vocational Teaching (2025– ), because the teacher in me still wants the formal craft

I call it being the student of my own course. It also means every tool I bring a client — Pandas, scikit-learn, TensorFlow, PyTorch, Power BI — arrives with an owner who can explain it in plain language and stand behind it.

Why people-first data wins here

Mpumalanga taught me something the textbooks won't: insight without trust is decoration. The shops, schools and clinics around Kabokweni and Nelspruit don't adopt data because a dashboard exists; they adopt it because someone walked them through it, in their language, patiently, twice. That patience is not soft skill decoration — it's the delivery mechanism of every insight I've ever sold or shared.

When I built the SATMED Health Care website for a real practice here at home, the technology took days. What made it work was knowing the patients: big buttons for calling, hours where people actually look, locations named the way locals say them. Data of people, for people.

The classroom is widening, but the job has never changed: take what's complicated, hand back something plain enough to act on by Monday.

Bring the complicated thing.

A mess of spreadsheets, a dashboard nobody opens, a website that whispers — I'll meet you where the confusion is.
