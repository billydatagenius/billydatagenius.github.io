Data Is a Language — and Your Business Already Speaks It

By Blessing "Billy" Bhobho — Founder, Billy Data Genius · Nelspruit, Mpumalanga

Every business in Kabokweni, Nelspruit or anywhere else keeps data. The sales book behind the counter. The WhatsApp orders. The month your stock mysteriously vanishes. The geniuses aren't the ones with the most data — they're the ones who read it.

A marksheet is never just marks

I spent fourteen years reading marksheets as a teacher of Accounting, Business Studies and Computer Applications Technology across Mpumalanga. A marksheet is a biography. It tells you which learner goes quiet after Section B, which topic the whole class pretended to understand, which term the wheels came off. A teacher who only sees "48%" sees a number. A teacher who reads the pattern sees a plan.

Your business numbers behave exactly the same way. Sales are not sales — they're a story about your customers: which days they trust you, which product they whisper about to their neighbours, which season they disappear. That story is being written in your books every single week, whether anyone reads it or not.

"Numbers that go unread
are just expensive paper."— Billy

Three steps any business can take this month

  • Collect on purpose. You don't need software to start — you need one row per day: date, what sold, for how much, to whom. Consistency beats sophistication. A WhatsApp log you actually keep beats a dashboard you abandon.
  • Read one number weekly. Pick a single question — "What actually made me money this week?" — and answer it in one chart. In my classrooms we asked one marksheet one question at a time. Same discipline, bigger stakes.
  • Act, then re-read. Data earns respect when it changes a decision — a price, a stock order, an opening hour. Then next week's numbers judge your action. That loop is the whole game.

The small-town advantage

Here's what I learned evaluating interactive boards in real classrooms (the study I completed in April 2026): technology doesn't create understanding — it removes friction for people who wanted to understand anyway. Local businesses have a short version of every dataset: fewer customers, cleaner signals, faster feedback. A Spaza shop owner can trace one insight to one decision in one afternoon. Corporates with huge warehouses of data would trade a limb for that clarity.

When I built the toolchain behind my analytics work — Power BI for the reading, Python with Pandas and scikit-learn for the heavy lifting — the goal was never fancy. The goal was: one business, one page, one clear decision.

The quiet part this unlocks

Once a business reads its own data, its confidence changes. It stops bidding for customers by guessing and starts recommending its own plans. That's when marketing gets sharper, websites start selling, and growth becomes a decision instead of a lucky season.
